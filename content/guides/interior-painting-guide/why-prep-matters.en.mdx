When someone sees a freshly painted room, they see the color. What they don't see are the hours of work that happened before the first tin was opened — and it's exactly those hours that decide whether the result looks professional or amateur.

Most of the flaws we see on walls — scratches, holes from picture frames or shelving, cracking at the corners where two walls meet — aren't actually a paint problem at all. They're a filling problem. Expensive paint applied over a surface like that doesn't hide the flaw — if anything, it often highlights it, especially if the paint has any sheen that catches light at an angle.

Sanding the old surface before filling serves a double purpose: it removes any loose flakes of old paint that would prevent proper adhesion, and it creates a slightly rough surface that the new material can grip onto. Skip this step, and even the best filler can flake off in chunks within a few months.

Priming, finally, is the step most DIYers skip because "it doesn't show." On new plaster, or on a wall going from a dark to a light color, primer evens out the surface's absorbency so the finish coat spreads evenly and only needs two coats instead of three or four.
