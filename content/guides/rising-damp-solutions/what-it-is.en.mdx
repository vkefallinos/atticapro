Rising damp isn't just "a bit of mold" — it's a physical phenomenon that follows specific rules, and precisely because it follows rules, it can be fixed for good once you understand how it actually works.

Every masonry wall — brick, stone or concrete — is riddled with microscopic pores. When a building's foundation sits in contact with damp ground, water is drawn into those pores and travels upward through the wall by the same mechanism that pulls oil up a lamp wick. Scientists call this capillary action: the narrower a material's pores, the higher moisture can climb, typically up to about a metre above ground level.

Buildings constructed in Greece before the 1980s rarely included a horizontal damp-proof membrane in their foundations — something considered standard in every new build today. That makes them especially vulnerable, particularly when combined with a high water table, poor rainwater drainage around the building, or low-permeability renders that trap moisture instead of letting it breathe.

It's important not to confuse rising damp with simpler problems: a leaking pipe, a cracked roof, or plain condensation from poor ventilation each have a completely different cause and a completely different fix. Getting the diagnosis right is the first and most critical step — treating the wrong problem just hides the symptom temporarily instead of solving it.
